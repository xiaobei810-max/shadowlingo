Stop Saying "Can I Have…?" — How to Order Food in Chinese

Translating "Can I have a coffee?" word for word gives 我可以要一杯咖啡吗？(wǒ kěyǐ yào yì bēi kāfēi ma?) — grammatically fine, but nobody orders like that. It sounds like you're asking permission. Locals just state what they want.

Just say 我要 or 来

What to sayPinyinMeaning
我要一杯咖啡wǒ yào yì bēi kāfēiI'll have a coffee
来一个这个lái yí ge zhègeone of these, please (pointing)
来两份炒面lái liǎng fèn chǎomiàntwo orders of fried noodles
我要这个wǒ yào zhègeI'll take this one

来 (lái) literally means "come," but for ordering it means "bring me / give me." It's friendly and completely normal — you'll hear it constantly at food stalls and restaurants.

Softening it (optional)

Add 请 (qǐng) or 谢谢 (xièxie) if you want to be extra polite: 来一杯美式，谢谢 (lái yì bēi měishì, xièxie) — "an Americano, thanks." But there's no need for the 可以…吗 permission frame. Direct isn't rude here — it's exactly how everyone orders.

Useful extras at the counter

  • 这个怎么做的？(zhège zěnme zuò de?) — "how is this made?" (asking about a dish)
  • 不要辣 (bú yào là) — "no spice"
  • 少放盐 (shǎo fàng yán) — "less salt"
  • 打包 (dǎbāo) — "to go / takeaway"
  • 买单 (mǎidān) — "the bill, please"
The shortcut — drop "can I have." Say 我要… or 来… plus quantity and item. Direct is exactly how everyone orders in China.
