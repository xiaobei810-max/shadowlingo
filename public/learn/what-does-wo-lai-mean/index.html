"Give Me Your Bag"? What 我来 Really Means in Chinese

The first time a Chinese friend or host reaches for your suitcase and says 给我 (gěi wǒ) or 我来我来 (wǒ lái wǒ lái), it can sound almost bossy in English — "give it to me," "I'll do it." It's the opposite. It's one of the warmest things they can do.

我来 = "let me get that"

我来 literally is "I come," but in daily life it means "let me do it / I've got this." People say it to take a task off your hands — carrying bags, pouring tea, grabbing the bill.

ExpressionPinyinMeaning
我来wǒ láiI'll do it / let me
我来我来wǒ lái wǒ láisaid twice, quickly, to insist warmly
我来拿wǒ lái nálet me carry that
我来吧wǒ lái bahere, let me

Why it sounds like a command (and isn't)

Chinese offers of help skip the "would you mind if I…" padding. Grabbing your bag and saying 给我 is care in action, not an order. The warmth is in the speed and certainty — they don't ask, they just step in.

If you want to decline politely: 不用不用，我自己来 (búyòng búyòng, wǒ zìjǐ lái) — "no need, I've got it myself."

Use it yourself

Say 我来 when you want to help someone and you'll sound both natural and kind. At a dinner table when someone's about to pour their own water — 我来 and reach for the bottle. Carrying boxes at a friend's place — 我来拿. It signals generosity without any extra words.

The shortcut — hear 我来 and 给我 as "let me help," not "give me." Say 我来 yourself when you want to help, and you'll sound genuinely warm.
