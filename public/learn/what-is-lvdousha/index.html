What Is 绿豆沙 (Lǜ Dòu Shā)? The Chinese Drink Called "Bean Sand"

Pick up a cup of 绿豆沙 (lǜ dòu shā) in a Chinese convenience store and the name reads, character by character, as "green bean sand." It's thick, pale green, faintly sweet — and there is no sand in it. That third character is doing something your dictionary probably didn't explain.

Breaking down the name

CharacterPinyinMeaning
绿豆lǜ dòumung bean
沙shāsand

沙 on its own really does mean sand — 沙子 (shāzi) is the stuff on a beach. But in the name of a food, 沙 isn't the material. It's the texture.

沙 in food is a texture, not an ingredient

When beans are simmered long enough, they stop being beans. They collapse into something fine, thick and soft — grainy the way powder is grainy, not the way gravel is. Chinese uses 沙 for exactly that state. So 绿豆沙 is "mung beans cooked until they go 沙."

It's the same logic behind English calling a texture "silky" or "velvety." Nobody thinks there's fabric in the soup.

The 沙 you'll see everywhere

ChinesePinyinMeaning
豆沙dòu shāsweet bean paste — the filling in 豆沙包 (dòu shā bāo)
红豆沙hóng dòu shāred bean paste, or the dessert soup version
冰沙bīng shā"ice sand" — a smoothie or slushie
沙冰shā bīngsame drink, characters reversed; both are common

冰沙 is the useful one. Walk into any bubble tea shop and you can order one by name.

What's actually in the cup

Bottled 绿豆沙 is usually 绿豆 blended with sugar and sometimes 莲子 (lián zǐ), lotus seed, and 百合 (bǎi hé), lily bulb — both common in Chinese sweet soups. It's served cold and drunk through most of the summer, because mung bean is traditionally considered cooling: a standard answer when someone is 上火 (shàng huǒ) or just overheated.

The soupier, thinner version of the same thing is 绿豆汤 (lǜ dòu tāng), mung bean soup. 汤 is soup; 沙 is the thick one.

The shortcut — 沙 in a food name means "cooked down fine and thick," never literal sand. Learn it once and you've got 豆沙, 红豆沙 and 冰沙 for free.
